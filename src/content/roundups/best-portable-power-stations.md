## The "capacity lie" that costs people $500

Every portable power station box has a number: "1,000Wh."

Here's what that number actually means, in the most honest terms possible:

**The station will deliver 1,000 watt-hours of energy if you run it at 100% load, continuously, with no idle draw, in a 25°C room.**

That's not how you use a power station. You run it at 30–60% load, with the inverter drawing 5–30W of idle power, in a garage that might be 40°C.

**The real delivered capacity:**

| Load | Inverter efficiency | Idle draw (hr) | Delivered Wh (from 1,000Wh) | % of advertised |
|------|-------------------|----------------|---------------------------|-----------------|
| 100% (1,000W) | 90% | 10W × 1hr = 10Wh | 900 − 10 = **890Wh** | 89% |
| 50% (500W) | 92% | 15W × 2hrs = 30Wh | 920 − 30 = **890Wh** | 89% |
| 30% (300W) | 90% | 20W × 3.3hrs = 66Wh | 900 − 66 = **834Wh** | 83% |
| 10% (100W) | 85% | 25W × 10hrs = 250Wh | 850 − 250 = **600Wh** | 60% |
| Idle (0W load) | — | 30W × 24hrs = 720Wh | 0 − 720 = **−720Wh** | **Depleted** |

**The pattern:** at low loads, the idle draw eats a disproportionate share of the battery. A 1,000Wh station running a 10W router all day (24 hours) delivers only 600Wh to the router — 60% of the advertised capacity. At 30% load, you get 83%. At 100% load, you get 89%.

This is the "capacity lie" in action. It's not a lie — it's a marketing choice. The advertised Wh is the **theoretical maximum at 100% load**, not the **realistic delivery at your actual load**.

## The honest sizing formula

**Total Wh needed = (sum of device watts × hours) ÷ 0.5**

The ÷ 0.5 accounts for inverter efficiency (85–92%) and idle draw. It's the single most important number in power station sizing, and it's the one most people skip.

### Worked example

You want to run, for 12 hours:
- Laptop: 60W × 6 hrs = 360Wh
- Wi-Fi router: 10W × 12 hrs = 120Wh
- LED lights: 10W × 8 hrs = 80Wh
- Phone charging: 10W × 4 hrs = 40Wh

**Total at 100% load: 600Wh**
**Divide by 0.5: 1,200Wh**

You need a **1,000Wh–1,500Wh station**. A 500Wh station will run for ~5 hours, not the 12 you need.

### Common mistakes

**Sizing for the devices, not the idle draw.** If you're running a 10W router all day, the idle draw (30W × 24hrs = 720Wh) is more than the router's consumption (240Wh). The router is 10W, but the inverter is drawing 30W to keep the outlet live.

**Buying a 3,600W inverter for a 1,000Wh station.** A 3,600W inverter has a 20–50W idle draw. If you only need 1,500W of output, buy a 1,500W inverter — the idle draw is 5–10W, not 20–50W. You save 400–1,200Wh per day of idle loss.

**Expecting a power station to replace a generator.** A 1,000Wh station is a **portable backup** for the essentials. It is not a whole-house generator. A whole house draws 5,000–10,000W continuously. You'd need a 5,000–10,000Wh station ($5,000–10,000) and a whole-house transfer switch.

## The picks, in detail

### #1: Jackery Explorer 1000 v2 — best overall

The sweet spot for camping, van life, and emergency backup. 1,000Wh runs a refrigerator for 8 hours, a laptop for 15 hours, and a Wi-Fi router for 20 hours. The 1,500W output handles a microwave (1,200W) and a hair dryer.

**The 12-minute solar fast charge** (with a 200W panel) is the feature that matters for off-grid use. You can go from 0 to 100% in 7 hours with solar, or 12 minutes with a 200W panel (the panel charges the station's built-in fast-charge circuit, not the battery directly).

**The 11,299 ratings on the original Jackery 1000** make it the most referenceable power station on Amazon. You can find a user review for almost any use case.

### #2: EcoFlow Delta 3 Classic — best for fast charging

The X-Stream charging technology is a genuine engineering advantage: 0–80% in 58 minutes on AC, which is 30% faster than the Jackery 1000 v2. For people who need to top up quickly (van life, overlanding, events), this is the feature that matters.

**The 1,800W output** handles a space heater (1,500W) and a microwave (1,200W) at the same time, which the Jackery 1,500W can't do.

### #3: Anker SOLIX C1000 Gen 2 — best for build quality

The Anker brand is the most trusted name in consumer power (chargers, power banks, car chargers). The SOLIX C1000 Gen 2 extends that trust to portable power: metal chassis, the most polished app, and the 1,087Wh capacity (the most of the three).

**The tradeoff:** it's $40 more than the Jackery and 4 lb heavier. You're paying for the Anker brand and the build quality.

### #4: Jackery Explorer 300 — best for day trips and CPAP

The 300Wh is not enough for a fridge or a microwave, but it's perfect for:
- Charging a phone, laptop, and tablet for a day trip
- Running a CPAP machine overnight (the #1 use case, and the reason for the 11,299 ratings)
- Emergency backup for a router and lights

At $199 and 2.9 lb, it's the entry point into the category. If you're not sure if you need a power station, buy this one first.

## The bottom line

For camping and emergency backup, the **Jackery Explorer 1000 v2** is the right buy: the right capacity, good charging speed, and the most user reviews to reference.

Buy the **EcoFlow Delta 3 Classic** if you need the fastest charging. Buy the **Anker SOLIX C1000 Gen 2** if you want the most premium build. Buy the **Jackery Explorer 300** if you're testing the waters or need a CPAP backup.

And use the honest sizing formula: **(sum of device watts × hours) ÷ 0.5**. The ÷ 0.5 is the difference between a power station that works and one that disappoints you at 2 AM when the lights go out.
