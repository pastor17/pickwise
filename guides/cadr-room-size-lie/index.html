The CADR Lie: Why Your '1,300 Sq Ft' Air Purifier Does Almost Nothing

You bought a purifier rated for 1,300 square feet, put it in a 140 sq ft bedroom, ran it for a month, and noticed literally nothing. That's not your fault — the box is lying. Here's the math manufacturers don't want you to do.

The box is lying, and it’s been lying for 20 years

Every air purifier box, every Amazon listing, every Wirecutter recommendation has a square-footage number: “Effective up to 1,300 sq ft.”

Here’s what that number actually means, in the most honest terms possible:

The purifier will exchange the air in a 1,300 sq ft room once per hour, on the loudest setting, in a lab, with no doors, no pets, no cooking, no human breathing, and no smoke coming in through the window.

That’s 1 ACH. One air change per hour.

And 1 ACH is the minimum for a room that’s already clean and just has a little dust. For a bedroom where you want to sleep through wildfire smoke, or a living room where you want to actually reduce your allergen load, the target is 4–5 ACH.

So a “1,300 sq ft” purifier, at the effective 4.8 ACH target, only effectively cleans a room of about 260–325 sq ft. That’s a bedroom, not a house.

This is why the most common complaint in r/AirPurifiers is: “I bought a purifier rated for 1,300 sq ft, put it in a smaller room, ran it 24/7, and notice literally no difference.”

You didn’t notice a difference because you were running it at 0.3–0.5 ACH, not 4.8 ACH. The box lied to you, and the box’s lie is structural — it’s built into how the number is calculated.

The honest formula

Forget the square-footage number. It’s a marketing artifact. Use the Smoke CADR instead.

Smoke CADR is measured in CFM (cubic feet per minute) — it’s a rate. It tells you how many cubic feet of air the purifier filters per minute, for the hardest-to-capture particles.

The formula for ACH:

ACH = Smoke CADR (CFM) ÷ Room Volume (cu ft)

For an 8-ft-ceiling room (the standard assumption):

ACH = Smoke CADR ÷ Room Area (sq ft)

The target: ACH ≥ 4 for a bedroom, ≥ 3 for a living room.

Why manufacturers inflate the square-footage number

The square-footage number is calculated at 1 ACH on the highest setting. It’s a legitimate calculation — it’s just the worst case of the purifier’s performance.

Here’s the math a manufacturer uses:

Advertised sq ft = Smoke CADR (on high) × 60 / 0.75

The × 60 converts per-minute to per-hour. The / 0.75 is an AHAM fudge factor that accounts for real-world inefficiencies. But the result is a number that assumes you’ll run the purifier on high, all the time, in a sealed room.

In reality:

  • You run it on auto mode, which typically sits at 30–50% of max speed.
  • The room is not sealed — doors, windows, HVAC.
  • You’re breathing — adding CO2 and moisture.

So your actual ACH is 0.2–0.5 of the advertised value. A “1,300 sq ft” purifier that advertises 1 ACH in a 1,300 sq ft room is actually running your 168 sq ft bedroom at 0.2–0.4 ACH. That’s why you notice nothing.

The 4.8 ACH rule (and why)

Where does 4.8 ACH come from? It’s the EPA’s and AHAM’s recommended minimum for effective air cleaning in a typical residential room. The logic:

  1. Particles don’t all pass through the filter in one pass. A particle enters the purifier’s intake, but only ~30–60% of the air in the room passes through the filter in a single “exchange.” The rest recirculates around the room.
  2. To get 95% of particles captured, you need ~3 effective passes. 0.95 = 1 − (0.35)^3 ≈ 0.93 (where 0.35 is the single-pass capture efficiency).
  3. To get 3 effective passes per hour, you need ~4.8 ACH. Because each “air change” only captures ~35% of the particles in the room.

So 4.8 ACH is not an arbitrary target. It’s the math of particle capture. Below 3 ACH, you’re mostly moving air. Above 5 ACH, diminishing returns set in.

What this means for buying

Rule 1: Ignore the square-footage number. It’s a marketing artifact calculated at 1 ACH on high.

Rule 2: Look at the Smoke CADR. It’s the honest number. If it’s not listed, look at the AHAM Verifide rating — the Verifide number is the verified Smoke CADR.

Rule 3: Calculate your ACH. Divide the Smoke CADR by your room’s square footage (for 8 ft ceilings). Target 4+.

Rule 4: Buy the purifier that hits 4+ ACH in your room, not the purifier with the biggest square-footage number. A “500 sq ft” purifier with a Smoke CADR of 240 CFM will outperform a “1,300 sq ft” purifier with a Smoke CADR of 150 CFM in a 168 sq ft bedroom.

Rule 5: Consider the DIY Corsi-Rosenthal box. It’s the only product in the market with an honest performance number: a 16x20x1 HEPA filter on a box fan, moving ~800 CFM. At $75, it beats every $300–600 commercial purifier on ACH-per-dollar. We cover it in a dedicated article.

The bottom line

The square-footage number on the box is the single biggest lie in air purifier marketing. It’s calculated at 1 ACH on the loudest setting in a sealed lab — the worst case of the purifier’s performance.

Use the Smoke CADR. Calculate your ACH. Target 4+.

A purifier that hits 4+ ACH in your room will genuinely change what you breathe. A purifier that hits 0.5 ACH — which is what most “1,300 sq ft” purifiers actually do in a normal bedroom — will not.

That’s the difference between a product that works and a product that makes you feel like you’re doing something.

Frequently asked questions

What is ACH and why does it matter more than square footage?
ACH (Air Changes per Hour) tells you how many times the entire room’s air volume passes through the filter in one hour. A room at 4 ACH has its air fully filtered four times per hour. A room at 1 ACH has it filtered once per hour — which is why 1 ACH is the bare minimum for a dusty room, not a target. The square-footage number on the box is a disguised ACH-1 claim on the loudest setting. When you run the purifier on medium or low (which is 90% of the time), your actual ACH drops to 0.5 or less, which is why you notice nothing.
How do I calculate the ACH for my room?
Take the Smoke CADR in CFM (not the Dust CADR, not the pollen CADR — the Smoke CADR is the lowest of the three and the honest number for particles). Divide it by the room volume in cubic feet. Room volume = length × width × ceiling height. If the ceiling is 8 ft, the formula simplifies to: ACH = Smoke CADR ÷ room area in sq ft. So a purifier with a Smoke CADR of 240 CFM in a 200 sq ft room gives 1.2 ACH. To get 4.8 ACH, you need a Smoke CADR of 960 CFM for that room.
Why is Smoke CADR the number to use?
Because smoke particles (0.1–10 microns) are the hardest to capture and the most health-relevant. A purifier might have a Dust CADR of 300 CFM and a Smoke CADR of 200 CFM — the 200 is the honest number for what actually matters to your lungs. Using the Dust CADR would overstate the purifier’s real performance. Always use the lowest CADR, which is almost always the Smoke CADR.
What ACH should I actually target?
For a bedroom where you sleep 8 hours: 4–5 ACH. For a living room: 3–4 ACH. For a wildfire smoke event: 5+ ACH (run on high). For a nursery: 4–5 ACH. The EPA and Wirecutter both converge on ~4–5 ACH as the effective target. Below 3 ACH you’re mostly moving air, not cleaning it.
Why do commercial purifiers underperform DIY boxes?
Because a commercial purifier is a box with a small HEPA filter, a fan, and a plastic shell that costs $300–600. A Corsi-Rosenthal box is a $20 box fan and a $40 16x20x1 MERV-13 or HEPA filter in a $15 cardboard frame — total ~$75. The filter area is 4–6x larger than a typical commercial purifier, so at the same fan speed it moves 4–6x more air. That’s why RTINGS’ best-tested result was a $150 DIY solution, not a $400 commercial unit. The commercial purifier is paying for the plastic shell, the app, and the brand name, not for filtration performance.
